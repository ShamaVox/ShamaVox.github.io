Why I started shamakeskar

For the last five years I've been a founding CTO, three times over. Each time I took a rough idea to a working product with real users: edge computer vision for ad measurement, a real-time sign language interpreter, and most recently an AI platform retailers paid for.

Every time, the same thing happened. The prototype came together fast. Making it reliable enough for paying customers was the real work, and it's the part most teams underestimate.

So I started shamakeskar. I work with founders who have a prototype or an early product that works, and I help them turn it into an MVP customers trust, then scale it to hundreds of customers. It's the same discipline I used for sixteen years at Google, LinkedIn and Amazon, now applied to AI.

Here's my most recent build.

A multi-agent orchestration platform I built myself, live with paying customers.

Before starting shamakeskar, I was cofounder and CTO of an AI-native wholesale platform for boutique retailers. I designed and built the whole system, from the first proof of concept to production.

Timeline
Proof of concept to MVP in four months, then production.
Stack
Python, FastAPI, Next.js, Postgres with pgvector, Redis, AWS, commercial LLM APIs, Datadog.

Supplier catalogs arrived in every format imaginable. Colors were abbreviated, size runs were written five different ways, and product images came in every size and resolution. Retailers were spending days retyping all of it into their store.

Getting a model to read a catalog was the easy part. The hard part was making every field correct, every time, on data nobody had seen before. So I built the checking before I built the features: a golden set of real examples, field-level accuracy tracking, and prompt and model versions logged with every output.

The work was split across specialized agents for extraction, sizing, quantities and color, coordinated by plain deterministic code rather than a framework. I chose models by how they scored against the golden set, never by how impressive a demo looked, and every failure could be traced to one place.

Ways to work together

Most teams I talk to have an LLM feature that works in the demo and breaks with real customers. That's the problem I solve first.

AI reliability sprint

Two to three weeks on one LLM feature that isn't holding up in production. Fixed scope and a fixed price, agreed before we start.

  1. Week 1Collect real failures from your logs and users, build an eval set from them, and measure your true first-pass success rate.
  2. Week 2Add validation, confidence scoring and bounded repair. Fix the failure types that cost you the most.
  3. Week 3Set up monitoring and thresholds, and document everything. Your engineers take it over with full ownership of the code.

You finish with a number you can track, a system that catches its own mistakes, and a clear view of what's left to fix.
